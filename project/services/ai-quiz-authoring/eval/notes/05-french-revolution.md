# Causes and early events of the French Revolution

The French Revolution began in 1789 and transformed France from an absolute monarchy into a country where political power was claimed in the name of the people. Historians usually group its causes into social, financial and intellectual ones, which together created a crisis that the government of King Louis XVI could not control.

French society before the Revolution, often called the Old Regime, was divided into three estates. The First Estate was the clergy and the Second Estate was the nobility. Together they made up only a small fraction of the population but owned a large share of the land and enjoyed important privileges, including exemption from most direct taxes. The Third Estate was everyone else, from wealthy merchants and lawyers to urban workers and the peasants who formed the great majority of the population. The Third Estate carried most of the tax burden while having little political influence.

The immediate trigger was a financial crisis. France had spent heavily on wars, including its support for the American colonists against Britain, and by the late 1780s a large part of royal spending went on paying interest on debt. Attempts by royal ministers to make the privileged estates pay more tax were blocked by the nobility. Bad harvests in 1788 then pushed up the price of bread, the main food of ordinary people, causing hunger and anger in the towns.

Ideas from the Enlightenment also played a part. Writers such as Rousseau and Montesquieu questioned the idea that kings ruled by divine right and argued for the separation of powers and for government based on the will of the people. These ideas spread through pamphlets, newspapers and discussion in salons and cafes, especially among the educated members of the Third Estate.

To solve the financial crisis, Louis XVI called a meeting of the Estates-General, which had not met since 1614. It opened at Versailles in May 1789. A dispute broke out at once over voting. Under the traditional rules each estate had one vote, so the clergy and nobility could always outvote the Third Estate. The Third Estate demanded voting by head instead.

In June 1789 the deputies of the Third Estate declared themselves the National Assembly, claiming to represent the nation. When they found themselves locked out of their usual meeting hall, they gathered in a nearby indoor tennis court and swore not to separate until France had a constitution. This promise became known as the Tennis Court Oath.

On 14 July 1789 crowds in Paris stormed the Bastille, a royal fortress and prison that had become a symbol of royal power. The crowd was looking above all for gunpowder and weapons. The date is still celebrated as the French national holiday. In August 1789 the Assembly abolished feudal privileges and adopted the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen, which stated that men are born and remain free and equal in rights.
