# Newton's laws of motion

Isaac Newton published three laws of motion in 1687 in his book Principia. Together they describe how forces change the motion of objects, and they remain accurate for everyday speeds and sizes. They stop working well for objects moving close to the speed of light and for very small particles, where relativity and quantum mechanics are needed instead.

A force is a push or a pull, measured in newtons (N). Force is a vector, which means it has both a size and a direction. When several forces act on an object, they combine into a single resultant force. If the forces cancel out, the resultant force is zero and the forces are said to be balanced.

Newton's first law says that an object stays at rest, or keeps moving at a constant speed in a straight line, unless a resultant force acts on it. This tendency to resist changes in motion is called inertia, and the more mass an object has, the greater its inertia. The first law explains why passengers lurch forward when a bus brakes suddenly: their bodies keep moving forward while the bus slows down.

Newton's second law links force, mass and acceleration. It is usually written as force equals mass times acceleration, or F = ma. One newton is the force needed to give a mass of one kilogram an acceleration of one metre per second squared. The law shows that the same force produces a smaller acceleration on a heavier object. For example, a resultant force of 10 N on a 2 kg trolley gives an acceleration of 5 metres per second squared, while the same force on a 5 kg trolley gives only 2 metres per second squared.

Weight is the force of gravity on an object. It is calculated as mass times the gravitational field strength, which is about 9.8 newtons per kilogram near the Earth's surface. Mass is measured in kilograms and stays the same everywhere, but weight changes from place to place. An astronaut on the Moon has the same mass as on Earth but a much smaller weight, because the Moon's gravitational field strength is only about one sixth of the Earth's.

Newton's third law says that when one object exerts a force on a second object, the second object exerts a force on the first that is equal in size and opposite in direction. These two forces always act on different objects, so they never cancel each other out. When you push against a wall, the wall pushes back on you with the same force. A rocket moves forward because it pushes exhaust gases backward, and the gases push the rocket forward.

Friction is a force that opposes motion between two surfaces in contact. Air resistance is a type of friction that acts on objects moving through air. When a skydiver jumps, the weight is at first larger than the air resistance, so the skydiver accelerates. As speed increases, air resistance grows until it equals the weight. The resultant force is then zero, and the skydiver falls at a steady speed called terminal velocity.
