# Photosynthesis

Photosynthesis is the process green plants, algae and some bacteria use to turn light energy into chemical energy stored in sugar. In plants it takes place inside chloroplasts, small organelles found mostly in the cells of the leaf. Each chloroplast contains stacks of flattened membrane sacs called thylakoids, surrounded by a fluid called the stroma.

The overall reaction can be summarised as six molecules of carbon dioxide plus six molecules of water, using light energy, produce one molecule of glucose and six molecules of oxygen. Carbon dioxide enters the leaf through tiny pores called stomata, mostly on the underside of the leaf. Water is absorbed by the roots and carried up to the leaves through the xylem.

The green colour of plants comes from chlorophyll, the main pigment that captures light. Chlorophyll absorbs red and blue light strongly and reflects green light, which is why leaves look green to us. Other pigments, such as carotenoids, absorb some of the wavelengths that chlorophyll misses and pass the energy on.

Photosynthesis happens in two linked stages. The first stage is the light-dependent reactions, which take place in the thylakoid membranes. Here light energy splits water molecules, a step called photolysis. Splitting water releases oxygen as a by-product, and this is the source of almost all the oxygen in the atmosphere. The energy captured in this stage is stored in two carrier molecules, ATP and NADPH.

The second stage is the Calvin cycle, sometimes called the light-independent reactions, which takes place in the stroma. In the Calvin cycle the enzyme RuBisCO fixes carbon dioxide by attaching it to a five-carbon sugar. Using the ATP and NADPH made in the first stage, the cycle builds three-carbon sugars, some of which are later joined to make glucose. Although the Calvin cycle does not use light directly, it stops soon after the light goes out because it runs out of ATP and NADPH.

Several factors limit the rate of photosynthesis. Light intensity matters up to a point; beyond that, adding more light does not speed the process up. Carbon dioxide concentration can also be limiting, which is why some commercial greenhouses add extra carbon dioxide to the air. Temperature affects the enzymes involved: the rate rises with warmth until the enzymes start to lose their shape, and then it falls sharply. Whichever factor is in shortest supply is called the limiting factor.

Plants use the glucose they make in several ways. Some is broken down in respiration to release energy for the cell. Some is converted into starch for storage, for example in potatoes, and some is turned into cellulose to build cell walls. Glucose is also used to make other substances such as proteins, once the plant has absorbed nitrates from the soil.

A simple classroom test for photosynthesis is the starch test. A leaf is boiled to break down its membranes, soaked in warm ethanol to remove the chlorophyll, and then covered with iodine solution. Areas that contained starch turn blue-black, while areas without starch stay orange-brown.
