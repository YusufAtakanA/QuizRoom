# Supply and demand

Supply and demand is the basic model economists use to explain how prices are set in a competitive market. A market is any arrangement where buyers and sellers trade a good or service, whether it is a street market for apples or an online market for used phones.

Demand is the quantity of a good that buyers are willing and able to buy at each possible price, over a given period. The law of demand says that, other things being equal, when the price of a good rises the quantity demanded falls. On a graph with price on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis, the demand curve therefore slopes downward from left to right.

Supply is the quantity that sellers are willing and able to offer at each price. The law of supply says that, other things being equal, a higher price leads to a larger quantity supplied, because producing more becomes more profitable. The supply curve therefore slopes upward from left to right.

The market reaches equilibrium at the price where the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied. This is where the two curves cross. If the price is set above equilibrium, sellers want to sell more than buyers want to buy, and the result is a surplus; sellers then tend to cut prices to clear their stock. If the price is below equilibrium, buyers want more than is available, causing a shortage, and the price tends to be pushed up.

It is important to separate a movement along a curve from a shift of the whole curve. A change in the good's own price causes a movement along the demand curve. A change in anything else that affects buyers shifts the whole demand curve. Demand shifts to the right when incomes rise for a normal good, when the price of a substitute rises, when the price of a complement falls, or when the good becomes more fashionable. For an inferior good, such as the cheapest own-brand bread, demand falls when incomes rise.

Supply also shifts. Lower production costs, better technology or more sellers entering the market shift the supply curve to the right. A bad harvest, higher wages or a new tax on producers shifts supply to the left. When supply shifts to the left and demand stays the same, the equilibrium price rises and the equilibrium quantity falls.

Price elasticity of demand measures how strongly the quantity demanded responds to a price change. It is calculated as the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price. Demand is called elastic when this value, ignoring the minus sign, is greater than one, and inelastic when it is less than one. Goods with few close substitutes, such as petrol or essential medicines, tend to have inelastic demand. Goods with many substitutes, such as one particular brand of crisps, tend to have elastic demand.

Governments sometimes set prices directly. A price ceiling is a legal maximum price; if it is set below equilibrium, it creates a shortage. A price floor is a legal minimum price, such as a minimum wage; if it is set above equilibrium, it creates a surplus.
